<summary>**DataDecide + Pretraining → Post-training**</summary>
	> *2026-08-18 13:34:58* Then I got sidetracked towards the DataDecide dataset, which seemed like an awesome source of variance which could make trying to predict behavior interesting. Also, the search for useful proxy metrics when trying to evaluate progress at such low scales seemed related. This became a project asking whether pretraining choices impact post-training success, even when pretrained final perf is held constant.
		The direction hit a wall when our post-training seemingly had no effect despite using standard procedures and datasets. Digging in further, and talking to others, we learned that this wasn’t easy to figure out from the way people presented their results, but it also was seen by others. Ultimately we ended that project a bit demoralized.
	**DataDecide Dataset** [DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf) by [Ian Magnusson](https://app.notion.com/p/d151e9c412524cac976d8bc7d4bdfaad) et al (Ai2, ICML 2025, arXiv 2504.11393) controlled pretraining experiments across 25 corpora (varying sources, deduplication, filtering) up to 100B tokens, model sizes up to 1B parameters, and 3 random seeds; they find that ranking models at a single small size (150M) predicts the best dataset at the 1B target scale \~80% of the time, and no scaling-law method among 8 baselines beats that simple baseline. Directly relevant to your proxy-metric interest: using continuous likelihood metrics as proxies in small experiments makes benchmarks including MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MBPP, and HumanEval \>80% predictable at the 1B scale with just 0.01% of the compute. This is exactly the “at low scale, accuracy is noise, so find a smoother observable” move.
	**Pretraining choices → posttraining success, with pretrain performance held constant**
	This became a hot topic in 2025–2026:
	- [Similar Models Learn Differently: Final-Window Pretraining Shapes Post-Training Beyond SFT](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f812cb778cf35b640d7a8) (arXiv 2607.25063, 2026) — closest to your exact experimental design: models that look similar after SFT diverge under identical post-training depending on late-pretraining data interventions. It explicitly cites Dohare’s plasticity paper, so your two original threads have formally merged.
	- [Echo Chamber: RL Post-training Amplifies Behaviors Learned in Pretraining](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816f9d14e85bc7839a54) ([Rosie Zhao](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81fda8aeec4636caa4ec), [Alexandru Meterez](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810e9cefc2deae8a54da) et al., COLM 2025; arXiv 2504.07912) — an end-to-end study that trains models from scratch on controlled pretraining mixtures, then compares PPO, GRPO, and Expert Iteration across scales. RL tends to amplify output patterns inherited from pretraining, while mixture- and scale-dependent settings include improvements, plateaus, and regressions. It also argues that controlled small-model proxies can yield real insight into RL behavior.
	- [Front-Loading Reasoning: The Synergy between Pretraining and Post-Training Data](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8169920ed64ce7c0ddea) ([Syeda Nahida Akter](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815f9ddef6104ed3e9c9) et al., NVIDIA, ICLR 2026; arXiv 2510.03264) — in controlled 8B experiments, broad and diverse reasoning data is most useful during pretraining, while SFT benefits more from a smaller curated set of high-quality long-CoT examples. Large mixed-quality SFT datasets can dilute the advantages created by reasoning-aware pretraining.
	- [Early Data Exposure Improves Robustness to Subsequent Fine-Tuning](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81bb8714ed6c14d9dd8c) ([Lawrence Feng](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81b3ad30c4f2bfafb2f4) et al., arXiv 2605.12705, 2026) finds that moving some target-domain data into pretraining improves retention after subsequent fine-tuning, even when immediate post-training performance is similar. [The Finetuner’s Fallacy: When to Pretrain with Your Finetuning Data](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118156f783c9abdf89) ([Christina Baek](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81e78c01d327cc5db046) et al., arXiv 2603.16177, 2026) supports the emerging view that early domain exposure can be more durable than introducing the same data only during fine-tuning, while the repetition schedule affects whether the model generalizes, overfits, or later forgets the domain.
	- [Understanding Reasoning from Pretraining to Post-Training](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81f98cddfbf646091feb) ([Jingyan Shen](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f819ebb85e87104b322ae) et al., arXiv 2607.16097, 2026) — lower pretraining loss strongly predicts higher post-RL pass@1 at fixed RL compute, while the local RL-improvement slope grows with log pretraining tokens. In a post-SFT auxiliary analysis, pretraining loss predicts large-k pass@k better than pass@1. This is in interesting tension with your original hypothesis because final pretraining loss carries strong predictive signal here, though not all of it.
	**Your “posttraining did nothing” wall — you were not alone**
	- [A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning: Pitfalls and Paths to Reproducibility](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f818ca12ff951377df50b) ([Andreas Hochlehnert](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81f6b486e678d8cc0f33) et al., COLM 2025; arXiv 2504.07086) — RL applied to distillation-based models yields little to no statistically significant gain in the tested settings; some promising original reports do not hold up under repeated evaluation, and RL-trained base models still often underperform instruction-tuned counterparts. Small benchmarks produce unstable estimates, making multiple seed runs essential. This documents exactly the ‘hard to figure out from how people present their results’ phenomenon you ran into.
	- [Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810abfbfccb7341fea12) ([Rulin Shao](https://app.notion.com/p/2846403706434f259c77b80b138c2f9c) et al., ICML 2026; arXiv 2506.10947) — on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, GRPO with random rewards improves MATH-500 by 21.4 points, nearly matching the gain from ground-truth rewards, while comparable spurious rewards generally fail outside Qwen families such as Llama 3.2 and OLMo 2. This supports the interpretation that some Qwen-centric RLVR gains reflect amplification of high-prior behaviors rather than generic reward learning.
	- [Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f819590c0d8d6ff4067ac) ([Yang Yue (乐洋)](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81a8b7d7f3c2d1572d0d) et al., NeurIPS 2025 oral; arXiv 2504.13837) — current RLVR methods often improve pass@k at small k but fail to expand the base model’s reasoning boundary at large k. [On the Limits of RLVR: Support, Entropy, and the Illusion of Reasoning](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81728d3ccf3ee573a1b9) ([Fang Wu](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8122af36e57f1052e3a1) and [Yejin Choi](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f812f89b9fd46a28010f8), AI for Math Workshop at ICML 2025) formalizes RLVR as predominantly support-preserving, entropy-reducing reweighting, while [Echo Chamber: RL Post-training Amplifies Behaviors Learned in Pretraining](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816f9d14e85bc7839a54) shows that post-training often amplifies patterns inherited from pretraining. The boundary is not absolute: [The Invisible Leash: Why RLVR May or May Not Escape Its Origin](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81999d39ff7dce4f94d8) (arXiv 2507.14843) distinguishes nominal from empirical support and allows finite-budget expansion even though shrinkage usually dominates, and [Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards Implicitly Incentivizes Correct Reasoning in Base LLMs](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8147b6d3e47b454cbcaa) (arXiv 2506.14245; ICLR 2026) reports counter-results under CoT-Pass@K and executable pass@K.
	- On the small-model side specifically: [Towards Revealing the Effectiveness of Small-Scale Fine-tuning in R1-style Reinforcement Learning](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f817e844bcd861034995a) ([Yutong Chen](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8107b32dc5a3941a37ad) et al., arXiv 2505.17988) shows that small-scale SFT on Qwen2.5-1.5B can reduce MATH-500 accuracy from 23.8% to 18.4% even while eliciting reasoning-style behaviors such as ‘Wait’ and ‘Let me check.’ [Through the Valley: Path to Effective Long CoT Training for Small Language Models](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815ebc24fad1ff1328ea) ([Renjie Luo](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815fa8c8d996a773abfd) et al., EMNLP 2025; arXiv 2506.07712) studies Long CoT Degradation in small language models and attributes the effect to error accumulation.
	**Retrospective reading of your project**
	You were testing “pretraining shapes post-training beyond final loss” at scales where (a) post-training gains are largely elicitation of capabilities your models didn’t yet have, (b) the Qwen confound was silently inflating the literature’s baseline expectations, and (c) benchmark noise swamps effect sizes without multi-seed evaluation. All three of those are now papers. Your negative result was, in hindsight, an early observation of a real phenomenon — the field just hadn’t built the vocabulary for it yet.
	And the connective tissue back to your original question: the field is converging on exactly your framing — final pretraining loss is an insufficient statistic for downstream success, and the open question is what *else* about the training trajectory (data order, late-window exposure, the “state of the learner” in plasticity terms) predicts post-training outcomes.

<summary>**WSD and Annealing Effects + Dataset Metrics/Features<br>    **→ Predict performance differences from dataset features.<br>    → “Does merging-as-annealing-proxy work on cosine mid-run checkpoints rather than just stable-phase ones?”<br>    → “Does a dataset’s “determinism profile” predict landscape geometry?”</summary>
	>
		*2026-08-18 17:03:03* I feel like the DataDecide ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf)) models would be more useful if they were trained with WSD learning rate schedule because then we could actually do comparable annealing tests along the course of the trajectory.  Unfortunately their evals are actually just showing the un-annealed or partially annealed performance directly after pretraining.  This skews all results including post-training performance.
		Then, what are some of our options to work around this problem? Are there ways to do small extensions of the training from each checkpoint to gather more data? Or, would redo-ing some portion of the pretraining using WSD enable some interesting experiments? How does related work interact with this question.
		Separately, how does the field currently quantify differences between datasets? They become approximately black boxes because they are SO large, but we analyze trained models which actually are black boxes, so there must be things we can do for datasets as well.
	**The annealing confound.** You’re right about the mechanics: DataDecide ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf))’s models were trained OLMo-style ([OLMo: Accelerating the Science of Language Models](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8124879cffe1021cc330)) with cosine schedules, so every intermediate checkpoint sits mid-schedule with high residual LR — in river-valley terms, evals on those checkpoints measure “position along river + current distance up the wall,” and the wall component is schedule-dependent noise relative to the question you care about. Post-training from such checkpoints inherits the confound (you’re fine-tuning from a point high on the wall). The relevant related work:
	- **Hägele et al. 2024** ([Scaling Laws and Compute-Optimal Training Beyond Fixed Training Durations](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811da333f746a38f3684)) made essentially your argument as a methodology proposal: constant LR + short cooldown matches cosine, so scaling-law and data experiments should use stable-phase runs with cheap decay branches instead of retraining full cosine runs per budget. 
	- The stable-checkpoint-plus-1-sqrt-decay-resume protocol is now standard in careful small-scale studies, and MiniCPM ([MiniCPM: Unveiling the Potential of Small Language Models with Scalable Training Strategies](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81d3bad5cbd562773738)) established the practical template — a stable-phase checkpoint plus a fixed-length decay matches full cosine baselines, with \~10% decay completing convergence and new data mixed in strictly during decay. 
	- Frontier labs use exactly this for data valuation (Llama 3 ([The Llama 3 Herd of Models](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81d8b591f5797b403be0))’s annealing-based data-quality assessments; Blakeney et al.’s “Does your data spark joy?” ([Does your data spark joy? Performance gains from domain upsampling at the end of training](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81ce81dcc7287955be73))). So “annealing branches as the correct eval” is validated practice — but no *open, multi-recipe suite* has it. 
	- **DataDecide-with-WSD-branches** ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf))** is a genuine gap**, and at 150M–300M scales redoing a subset of recipes is affordable; you’d be producing the dataset the field’s own methodology papers say should exist.
	**Two cheap partial fixes on the *existing* checkpoints**. 
	- **Checkpoint merging as pseudo-annealing**
		- Merging recent checkpoints ([WSM: Decay-Free Learning Rate Schedule via Checkpoint Merging for LLM Pre-training](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816abd01d38ba73e1c77)) with weights derived from an emulated decay curve provides a robust annealed model without ever altering the live learning rate
		- WSM-merged models ([WSM: Decay-Free Learning Rate Schedule via Checkpoint Merging for LLM Pre-training](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816abd01d38ba73e1c77)) consistently and closely mirror the results of a true LR anneal at intermediate stages of long runs. 
		- Production models now use this as their mid-run readout: Nemotron 3 ([Nemotron 3 Super: Open, Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Hybrid Mamba-Transformer Model for Agentic Reasoning](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f817db04de2282ca800d8)) applies sliding-window checkpoint merging to get stronger quality readouts without dedicated decay runs, estimating savings of \~16% of total pretraining FLOPs. 
		- **→ The open question — whether merging-as-annealing-proxy works on *cosine* mid-run checkpoints (varying LR within the merge window) rather than stable-phase ones — is itself a nice small experiment, and if it works even approximately you can retrofit “annealed” evals onto all of DataDecide for the cost of evals. **
	- **The multi-power law** ([A Multi-Power Law for Loss Curve Prediction Across Learning Rate Schedules](https://app.notion.com/p/29ba77d7fdf949e1a1f9bdb4df8c35a4))** predicts the loss drop from a hypothetical decay given the trajectory so far, so you could correct unannealed loss curves analytically.** 
		- It won’t give you downstream metrics, but it quantifies how much each recipe’s apparent ranking is schedule artifact. 
		- One caveat to your framing: DataDecide ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf)) found intermediate-checkpoint *decisions* matched compute-equivalent final checkpoints — so the confound may partially cancel for rankings even while distorting levels and post-training. 
		- Testing when it cancels vs. doesn’t is exactly what your WSD branches would settle. 
		- Also note the river-valley theory ([Understanding Warmup-Stable-Decay Learning Rates: A River Valley Loss Landscape View](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e)) predicts the decay phase itself makes progress along the river — so annealed evals aren’t a pure “reveal” either; branch length becomes a parameter to control.
	**Dataset featurization.** Your framing — datasets aren’t black boxes, just obscured by size — matches where the field is, and the current toolkit splits into three families:
	1. **Model-mediated features** (dominant, works best, philosophically unsatisfying for you): describe data by how models respond to it. Perplexity correlations ([Improving Pretraining Data Using Perplexity Correlations](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81eda703dfcb3298681d)) uses losses of 90 public LLMs on tens of thousands of web domains as features, exploiting the observation that losses on pretraining texts correlate with downstream benchmark performance; RegMix ([RegMix: Data Mixture as Regression for Language Model Pre-training](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81289b7ed3c13fb9f346)) formulates mixture selection as a regression task from small proxy runs, matching or beating DoReMi ([DoReMi: Optimizing Data Mixtures Speeds Up Language Model Pretraining](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81ad971eea97f46d750e)) with \~10% of the compute; and data mixing laws ([Data Mixing Laws: Optimizing Data Mixtures by Predicting Language Modeling Performance](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8184a396c885d7adc8b7)) (BiMix ([BiMix: A Bivariate Data Mixing Law for Language Model Pretraining](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814e8a35e7e86ba02efa)) etc.) fit parametric laws jointly over domain proportion and data volume. These predict outcomes well but featurize the dataset as “mixture weights over named domains” — they don’t tell you *what property* of the data mattered.
	2. **Dataset-similarity embeddings**: Task2Vec-based ([Task2Vec: Task Embedding for Meta-Learning](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811694d9c6ff7b5946e6)) alignment coefficients ([Quantifying the Importance of Data Alignment in Downstream Model Performance](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810b8e84d0c64846d5a5)) quantify similarity between two datasets and, in controlled interventional experiments, measure alignment’s impact on downstream performance. Related: the diversity coefficient (Miranda et al.) ([Beyond Scale: The Diversity Coefficient as a Data Quality Metric for Variability in Natural Language Data](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81039425cc7740bd7220)) — expected Task2Vec distance between batches — which correlates with downstream quality, and FID-style dataset distances in other modalities. Important negative result to respect: data similarity alone is not enough to explain language model performance ([Data Similarity is Not Enough to Explain Language Model Performance](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81a3ab0de58e2a85ea63)).
	3. **Intrinsic corpus statistics** (closest to your instinct, least developed): WIMBD (Elazar et al.) ([What's In My Big Data?](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8155bd6ee458dbcf713c)) computes corpus-level statistics — duplication, contamination, domain composition, length distributions — at trillion-token scale, proving the “obscured by size” problem is tractable; compression-based measures (gzip ratio, entropy-law style) as scalar complexity/redundancy features; Zipf/burstiness/type-token statistics — which, recall, are exactly the properties Chan et al. ([Data Distributional Properties Drive Emergent In-Context Learning in Transformers](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814f827bd8a3c57b7e60)) showed *cause* ICL emergence in small transformers. That last link matters: the one place intrinsic data statistics have been causally tied to a capability is your Chapter-2 setting.
	**Open Question: Intrinsic Features Impact on Controlled Outcomes.**
	- DataDecide ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf)) hands you a supervised problem — 25 corpora with measured outcomes (and \~300 pairwise decisions, plus per-task breakdowns).
	- No one has systematically featurized those 25 corpora (WIMBD statistics ([What's In My Big Data?](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8155bd6ee458dbcf713c)), diversity coefficient ([Beyond Scale: The Diversity Coefficient as a Data Quality Metric for Variability in Natural Language Data](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81039425cc7740bd7220)), compression ratios, perplexity-correlation profiles ([Improving Pretraining Data Using Perplexity Correlations](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81eda703dfcb3298681d))) and asked which features predict the outcome table, or whether intrinsic features match model-mediated ones.
	**Open Question: Does a dataset’s “determinism profile” predict landscape geometry?**
	- The river-valley hypothesis ([Understanding Warmup-Stable-Decay Learning Rates: A River Valley Loss Landscape View](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e)) **attributes the valley geometry itself to data properties** — **deterministic tokens forming the river**, **uncertain tokens the walls** — meaning a dataset’s “determinism profile” (cheap to estimate with any reference model) is a candidate feature predicting not just performance but *landscape geometry*, i.e., annealing behavior. 
	- That would tie your WSD-branch suite and your featurization question into one design: features of the data predicting both where the river goes and how much the decay reveals.

<summary>**Mapping Dataset Tokens into Deterministic vs Uncertain (river-valley test** ([Understanding Warmup-Stable-Decay Learning Rates: A River Valley Loss Landscape View](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e))**)<br>    → “Validate river-valley theory implications on token loss behavior:<br>              **Each annealing branch tells you, token by token, how much loss drops under decay = an empirical “hillside-ness” <br>                 score at that point in training (decay-responsive = wall, decay-inert = already at the river).<br>             Branch repeatedly along the stable phase and you get the trajectory of the mapping: which tokens migrate <br>                 from decay-responsive to decay-inert, at what rate. And also whether different pretraining corpora produce<br>                 different migration dynamics for the *same* held-out tokens.<br>             Cross that with the epistemic/aleatoric decomposition (aleatoric estimated from an ensemble) and validate:<br>                    1. decay-responsiveness should track epistemic-but-not-aleatoric uncertainty.<br>                    2. datasets should differ in their epistemic-drainage schedules rather than their aleatoric floors.”</summary>
	>
		*2026-08-18 17:33:25* When considering the Wen et al interpolation signal “river test” ([Understanding Warmup-Stable-Decay Learning Rates: A River Valley Loss Landscape View](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e)) they describe tokens as having different effects, “highly deterministic tokens (facts, knowledge) contribute the river direction, while uncertain, ambiguous tokens create the steep hillsides.” Have there been investigations into mapping data tokens into the two effect buckets and/or looking at how that mapping changes over training?
	The mapping has been made statically, and there are fragments of the dynamics, but the full “watch the bucket assignment evolve over training” study doesn’t exist.
	**Wen et al.** ([Understanding Warmup-Stable-Decay Learning Rates: A River Valley Loss Landscape View](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e))** did the static version themselves, plus one dynamic observation.** They didn’t just conjecture the mechanism — they built a toy bigram language (cities with name distributions of varying determinism), showed it reproduces the river-valley loss geometry, and demonstrated that the stable learning-rate phase learns the deterministic tokens whereas the decay phase learns better the stochastic tokens. On real data they validated the mapping correlationally: a significant Spearman correlation (\~0.39) between token-level uncertainty and local sharpness of the landscape. So the bucket→geometry link exists, and there’s one coarse dynamic fact: the two buckets are learned in different *phases*. What they didn’t do is track individual tokens’ bucket membership as a function of training time — the mapping is treated as a fixed property of the data.
	**The dynamics exist in adjacent literatures under different names.** 
	“Deterministic vs. Uncertain” is really two different things, and only one of them can change over training. The uncertainty-decomposition line makes this precise: 
	- Token-level uncertainty ([Token-Level Uncertainty-Aware Objective for Language Model Post-Training](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f818bbed9d95dddb46f4c)) splits into epistemic (reducible; the model hasn’t learned it yet) and aleatoric (irreducible ambiguity in language), these vary enormously across tokens, and epistemic uncertainty decreases faster for low-aleatoric examples as training progresses. 
	- In river-valley terms ([Understanding Warmup-Stable-Decay Learning Rates: A River Valley Loss Landscape View](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e)): aleatoric uncertainty is the *true* hillside (a data property, fixed), while epistemic uncertainty is distance-not-yet-traveled along the river — and a token’s *apparent* bucket (measured by current loss/entropy) migrates as epistemic uncertainty drains away. 
	- So the “mapping changing over training” is largely the epistemic component collapsing at token-dependent rates, and that paper is the closest existing measurement of it, though it never connects to landscape geometry.
	The other fragment is the Rho-1 / selective-language-modeling line (“Not All Tokens Are What You Need”) ([Not All Tokens Are What You Need for Pretraining](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8135874fdbbe330e70d5)), which classified tokens by their *loss trajectories* across pretraining checkpoints
	- persistently-high, persistently-low, descending, and (notably) fluctuating/ascending categories
	- finding that only a minority of tokens show meaningful loss descent late in training. 
	- That’s literally a token-bucket-over-time taxonomy, built for data-selection purposes with no landscape interpretation attached. 
	And on the post-training side:
	- the entropy-token line ([Revisiting Entropy in Reinforcement Learning for Large Reasoning Models](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8137a724e7601f1a87e4)) found that masking RLVR updates to different token regimes produces qualitatively different training dynamics — with certain regimes driving stability and others driving collapse
	- the related “forking tokens” result ([Beyond the 80/20 Rule: High-Entropy Minority Tokens Drive Effective Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81faaf10d0ea83517e29)) that a small high-entropy minority of tokens carries most of RLVR’s effect. 
	- That’s your hillside bucket resurfacing as the locus of post-training, which is a suggestive bridge nobody has drawn explicitly: the tokens that form the valley walls in pretraining look like the same tokens where RL does its work.

	**Same checkpoint, different eval seeds: mostly deterministic — which is both good news and the key subtlety.** Decompose “eval noise” into its actual sources: (a) *Inference nondeterminism* — for the loglikelihood/rank-classification evals DataDecide ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf)) uses (OLMES-style ([OLMES: A Standard for Language Model Evaluations](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814690a8f496334f818e))), there’s no sampling; the only randomness is floating-point non-associativity from batching/kernel scheduling, which can flip a rare borderline item but is typically negligible. Re-evaling the same checkpoint with a different “seed” gives you nearly identical numbers. (b) *Generation-based evals* (anything sampled at T\>0) do vary with seed — but that’s not most of the DataDecide ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf)) suite. (c) *Configuration variance* — few-shot demo choice, demo order, prompt template. This is large, but it’s *systematic*, not random: it shifts all models coherently, so it’s a bias axis to sweep, not noise to average. The upshot: you can’t buy variance reduction on a fixed checkpoint by re-evaling with new seeds, because almost all of the variance you care about isn’t in the eval — it’s in *training* (seed, data order, init). That’s exactly how Signal and Noise ([Signal and Noise: A Framework for Reducing Uncertainty in Language Model Evaluation](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e)) carves it: their noise is the model’s own step-to-step wander, a benchmark’s sensitivity to random variability between training steps, not measurement error at a fixed point.
		Which sharpens your n=3 worry correctly: 3 training seeds is indeed too few to estimate per-recipe seed variance directly. Three mitigations, all cheap. First, **pool**: assume (and then test) that seed-variance is roughly homoscedastic across recipes at fixed scale — 25 recipes × 3 seeds gives you \~50 degrees of freedom for a *shared* variance estimate, plenty, and the test for heteroscedasticity across recipes is itself interesting (a recipe whose seeds diverge more is a finding, not a nuisance). Second, **use the trajectory as replicate** — this is Signal and Noise’s own trick ([Signal and Noise: A Framework for Reducing Uncertainty in Language Model Evaluation](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e)): variability across a window of late checkpoints within one run approximates run-to-run noise at a fraction of the cost, and it’s exactly the quantity your drift/diffusion decomposition would put on principled footing (their windowed noise estimate assumes the drift within the window is negligible; your decomposition checks and corrects that assumption). Third, **bootstrap over items** for the orthogonal axis — benchmark-composition uncertainty from a single run — which costs nothing and is routinely omitted. With those three, n=3 stops being a blocker for anything except recipe-specific variance claims, which you’d flag as the one thing the suite genuinely can’t support.
	**IRT on the DataDecide matrix: yes, and you’ve spotted a real opening.** The setup is ideal: hundreds of model×checkpoint rows, thousands of item columns, binary (and margin) outcomes — precisely the data shape item response theory was built for. Prior art exists but hasn’t been pointed here: IRT for NLP benchmarks (Lalor et al. ([Building an Evaluation Scale using Item Response Theory](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81588b16ffb2c1ea8cb4)); Rodriguez et al.’s “Evaluation Examples Are Not Equally Informative” ([Evaluation Examples are not Equally Informative: How should that change NLP Leaderboards?](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8148b63efdfe60dce3e1))), tinyBenchmarks ([tinyBenchmarks: evaluating LLMs with fewer examples](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81888d3cf04f3ec4a02b)) (Polo et al. — IRT-selected \~100-item subsets that preserve full-benchmark rankings), and metabench ([metabench -- A Sparse Benchmark of Reasoning and Knowledge in Large Language Models](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811b959bc0ebffa296c6)). What they all did is fit IRT to *diverse converged models* to compress benchmarks. What the DataDecide setting ([DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments](https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf)) adds is *structure in the model axis*: the rows aren’t arbitrary models, they’re organized trajectories (recipe × scale × seed × step), which converts IRT from a compression tool into a measurement instrument for your program:
	- **Ability θ(t) as the movement metric.** IRT’s latent ability is a continuous scalar extracted from binary outcomes, pooling information across items weighted by their discrimination — it should be substantially lower-noise than accuracy on the same items, i.e., a principled version of the Signal-and-Noise ([Signal and Noise: A Framework for Reducing Uncertainty in Language Model Evaluation](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e)) intervention of switching to better-signal metrics and filtering noisy subtasks (their filtering is implicit zero/one weighting; IRT estimates the weights). Testable immediately: is SNR(θ trajectories) \> SNR(accuracy trajectories) on their own released data?
	- **Differential item functioning (DIF) is your matched-loss comparison, formalized.** DIF asks: do items behave differently across model groups *at the same ability level*? Substitute “recipe” for group and you have the psychometric statement of “pretraining data shapes models beyond final performance.” Items exhibiting recipe-DIF are the loci where recipes differ irreducibly — and the natural follow-up is whether DIF items cluster by your token-determinism buckets or by domain, connecting the eval layer back to the data-featurization layer.
	- **Item characteristic curves against compute give per-item emergence points** — the θ (or loss) at which each item becomes solvable — turning “emergence” from a benchmark-level claim into a distribution over items, and giving your proxy-metrics thread a per-item version of the loss-to-accuracy mapping.
	- **Dimensionality is the honest first check, and it’s substantive rather than procedural:** classical IRT assumes one latent dimension; if a single θ fits the DataDecide matrix well, that’s evidence recipes at these scales differ mostly along one axis (and matched-loss ≈ matched-everything, deflating part of your hypothesis at this scale!); if it demands multiple dimensions, the factor structure *is* the answer to “what do recipes change besides final performance.” Either outcome is a result.
	Two cautions: 
	- IRT’s local-independence assumption is violated by shared-passage items and by contamination (fit diagnostics will flag these — also useful); 
	- binary IRT discards the margin information that carries most small-scale signal, so fit both binary and continuous-response variants (on likelihood margins) and compare
	- the comparison itself replicating Signal and Noise’s metric-choice finding inside a single framework.
	- As a project shape, this is another all-inference, public-data, weeks-scale artifact: “a psychometric reanalysis of DataDecide” slots between your Stage-0 movement decomposition and the recipe questions, and produces the calibrated instrument (θ trajectories, item bank with difficulty/discrimination/DIF flags) that every later stage would rather use than raw accuracy.
