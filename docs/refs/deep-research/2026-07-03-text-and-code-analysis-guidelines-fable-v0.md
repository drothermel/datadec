# Text and Code Analysis Deep Research Guidelines

These guidelines are read alongside the general Deep Research Session Guidelines and a per-report brief. The general guidelines govern how a landscape survey is conducted and written; this document defines what counts as an entry in research on text and code analysis; the per-report brief defines the specific subject, scope, and coverage requirements of the report at hand.

## 1. Method Boundaries

**Small pretrained and adaptable models are in scope.** Models small enough to inspect, retrain, and reason about are valid entries: part-of-speech taggers, named-entity recognizers, dependency parsers, small classifiers, topic models with fixed vocabularies, and similar. Approaches that describe retraining or fine-tuning on the reader's own corpus as part of normal use are in scope on the same terms.

**Large generative language models are out of scope.** Running a generative language model over an input to extract features, judge similarity, or otherwise analyze it — whether locally or through an API — is not an entry. Two independent tests apply, and failing either excludes the approach: the model must be small enough to inspect, retrain, and reason about rather than treated as a generative black box; and its outputs must be drawn from a fixed schema (tags from a tagset, labels from a label set, spans over the input) rather than generated freely.

**Learned dense representations are out of scope; algorithmically transparent sketches are not.** Methods whose primary output is a learned dense vector with no individually interpretable dimensions — sentence embeddings, code embeddings, neural representation methods such as code2vec or ASTNN — are not entries, even when the underlying model is small. The distinguishing property is that the representation is learned and opaque, not that it is a vector: mechanically derived signatures such as MinHash, SimHash, and other hashing or fingerprinting schemes are in scope, because the construction is fully inspectable and the meaning of a match or collision is provable from the algorithm.

**Manual and do-it-yourself approaches are not entries.** Hand-written regex pipelines, hand-picked feature recipes, and extraction procedures the reader would assemble themselves are not entries in the landscape, and a DIY route is never presented as an alternative to a surveyed entry. Shipped standard-library modules (such as Python's ast, tokenize, and dis) count as libraries and receive entries on normal terms; recipes built on top of them ("here is what you would extract by walking the tree yourself") do not. If an entry is thin enough that a reader could plausibly reimplement it, that is a fact worth stating, not grounds for substituting a recipe.

## 2. Coverage Requirements

**Entries are not restricted to any one language or ecosystem.** Text and code analysis tooling spans C, C++, Rust, Java, OCaml, and Haskell ecosystems as well as Python, and an entry need not be Python-native or pip-installable to qualify. When the inputs of interest include non-Python content — SQL, shell, JSON, YAML, Markdown structure, other programming languages — tools that handle those languages are first-class entries. Multi-language tools are described in their full generality, not solely through the lens of one language binding.

**Research artifacts are entries on equal terms.** As a concrete instantiation of the general equal-terms principle: an approach that requires cloning a research repository, reading the associated paper, and running a training script is described on the same terms as an approach that installs with one command. The entry material is what the artifact extracts or produces, drawn from the paper and reference implementation when no library documentation exists.

**Legacy is a factual label with stated evidence, and the only judgment-adjacent status label in use.** An entry is marked Legacy on concrete evidence of unmaintained status: the most recent release is more than roughly two years old, the primary maintainer has publicly stated the project is unmaintained, the repository has been archived or marked read-only, or the project's own documentation directs users to a successor. Any one of these suffices, and the specific evidence is stated alongside the label. Legacy is distinct from superseded: an entry with active releases that is less popular than a competitor is not Legacy, and per the general guidelines' isolation rule, superseded-by framing does not appear in descriptions at all. Legacy entries are described more briefly but never below the descriptive schema floor.

**Speed facts, not speed scores.** Instantiating the general rule against engineering-variable ranking axes: out-of-the-box samples-per-second reflects reference-implementation quality, not the approach itself, and is never used to compare entries. Latency, model size, and computational cost appear only as descriptive facts when they distinguish an entry substantively — for example, that an approach runs a small classical model rather than a neural one.

## 3. Per-Report Brief

Each report is commissioned with a brief that states the report's subject, its scope boundaries, and an explicit coverage list naming the tool families or areas the report must address. The coverage list is a floor, not a menu: every named area must be surveyed, additional areas discovered during research are included, and the specific entries within an area are determined by the landscape itself rather than by the brief.
