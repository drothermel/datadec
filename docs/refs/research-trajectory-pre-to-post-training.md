<summary>**Continual Learning: Plasticity + Multi-Power Laws**</summary>
	> *2026-08-18 13:21:08 *During a previous research project i was interested in the continual learning space and specifically in whether features of a loss curve could be used to estimate or predict certain properties relevant to “success” however that was defined. I started out with the work by Sutton’s student and Clare Lyle about plasticity (considering experiments on the scale of CIFAR-10) and somehow ended up at llm pretraining and papers like the Multi-Power law paper that predicts test loss from train loss and downstream accuracy from test loss.
	**The plasticity thread (continual learning / RL, small-scale)**
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c2de135cd1f8034bc8ed1e80221f6b8"/>  et al., <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/a3e975b6036d4d729bed0d594de740e0"/> (Nature 2024; earlier arXiv version “Maintaining Plasticity…”, 2306.13812) — Sutton’s student is <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c2de135cd1f8034bc8ed1e80221f6b8"/> . They show that standard deep-learning methods gradually lose plasticity in continual-learning settings until they learn no better than a shallow network, demonstrated on ImageNet (repurposed as task sequences) and RL problems, and propose continual backpropagation (selectively reinitializing dormant/unuseful units during training). The incremental-CIFAR experiments you remember are in their codebase.
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c2de135cd1f80f9a315ff7bbdf9894e"/>  et al., <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c2de135cd1f809ebd9fe2463f494efd"/>  (ICML 2023, arXiv 2303.01486) — a systematic empirical analysis finding plasticity loss is deeply connected to changes in loss-landscape curvature, often occurring without saturated units; and the follow-up <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/6bde1d83f6de4c56ba273e25fb0aaf2b"/>  (arXiv 2402.18762).
	**The LLM loss-curve-prediction thread (pretraining, large-scale)**
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f2491f5a6e1a412eb4bced7331c50c4f"/>  et al., <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/29ba77d7fdf949e1a1f9bdb4df8c35a4"/>  (arXiv 2503.12811, ICLR 2025) — predicts the full pretraining loss curve at every intermediate step across LR schedules, using a power law on the sum of learning rates plus extra power-law terms for the decay-induced loss drop; fitted on a few runs, it extrapolates to unseen schedules and even discovers a schedule beating cosine (resembling WSD).
	- For loss → downstream accuracy: <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/756ac5715d434d17a62fae97caf17189"/> et al., <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/59133e03cc774153b83012321d143718"/>  (arXiv 2410.08527) — the two-stage “FLP” pipeline: FLOPs → pretraining loss → downstream performance. Related: <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/9ef7ea1367cf4c2b9c916bfe173265c6"/>  et al. 2024 (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/0be09c486c0d4cb4a37823b841b48c63"/> ), where downstream accuracy is predicted as an exponential function of training loss, and <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/64073767dcbb48d48b51bb5ce1ec6ed1"/>  et al.’s model-ladders paper (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/a46c70a5768e4964b656aeb958b2ed8a"/> ) which maps compute → task NLL → accuracy.
	**The link between the subcommunities**
	Both are doing the same epistemic move: treating the loss curve (or statistics derivable during training) as a *measurable signal that predicts a latent capability you actually care about*. In the plasticity literature, the latent quantity is future trainability — can the network still reduce loss on the *next* task? Lyle’s work explicitly hunts for cheap-to-compute training statistics (curvature, feature rank, dead units, weight norm) that correlate with or cause that ability. In the scaling-law literature, the latent quantity is final loss or benchmark accuracy, predicted from early/partial loss curves or small-model runs. Both are also fundamentally about *optimization dynamics under non-stationarity*: LR decay in pretraining is itself a controlled non-stationarity, and the multi-power law’s decay term is essentially modeling how the optimizer’s response to the schedule shapes the curve — a dynamics question the plasticity people would recognize.
	There’s also a growing literal bridge: papers now ask whether plasticity loss appears in LLM-scale training (e.g., <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/41d682e64893426a922671aa41c11ec7"/> et al <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/6b9dcac7ded64529b9b29fd319075b9d"/> ), which matters for continual pretraining and mid-training regimes — exactly where the two threads collide.
	**Core differences**
	- *Regime:* plasticity work assumes an explicitly non-stationary data stream (task sequences, RL bootstrapping); scaling-law work assumes a single stationary distribution where the only “non-stationarity” is the LR schedule.
	- *Target of prediction:* plasticity work predicts a property of the *learner* (future adaptability); scaling work predicts a property of the *outcome* (final loss/accuracy). Success in one is “can it keep learning,” in the other “how good will it be when we stop.”
	- *Methodological flavor:* plasticity is mechanistic/causal — intervening on curvature, resets, normalization to see what restores learning. Scaling laws are phenomenological — fit a parametric form, extrapolate, and mostly stay agnostic about mechanism.
	- *Scale and stakes:* CIFAR/ImageNet/Atari with many cheap seeds vs. Llama-scale runs where the whole point is that you can’t afford to run the experiment, so prediction substitutes for experimentation.
	If you want a “loss-curve features → success” framing that unifies them, the shared question is: what low-dimensional summary of training dynamics is sufficient to forecast a capability? The plasticity answer so far is “no single statistic — curvature comes closest” (Lyle), while the pretraining answer is “a surprisingly simple functional of the LR schedule” (multi-power law) plus a sigmoid/exponential link to accuracy — with the caveat that hard accuracy metrics can look emergent, showing no progress above chance until the loss crosses a threshold, which is where the loss-to-accuracy mapping gets fragile.

<summary>**DataDecide + Pretraining → Post-training**</summary>
	> *2026-08-18 13:34:58* Then I got sidetracked towards the DataDecide dataset, which seemed like an awesome source of variance which could make trying to predict behavior interesting. Also, the search for useful proxy metrics when trying to evaluate progress at such low scales seemed related. This became a project asking whether pretraining choices impact post-training success, even when pretrained final perf is held constant.
		The direction hit a wall when our post-training seemingly had no effect despite using standard procedures and datasets. Digging in further, and talking to others, we learned that this wasn’t easy to figure out from the way people presented their results, but it also was seen by others. Ultimately we ended that project a bit demoralized.
	**DataDecide Dataset** <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/> by <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/d151e9c412524cac976d8bc7d4bdfaad"/> et al (Ai2, ICML 2025, arXiv 2504.11393) controlled pretraining experiments across 25 corpora (varying sources, deduplication, filtering) up to 100B tokens, model sizes up to 1B parameters, and 3 random seeds; they find that ranking models at a single small size (150M) predicts the best dataset at the 1B target scale \~80% of the time, and no scaling-law method among 8 baselines beats that simple baseline. Directly relevant to your proxy-metric interest: using continuous likelihood metrics as proxies in small experiments makes benchmarks including MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MBPP, and HumanEval \>80% predictable at the 1B scale with just 0.01% of the compute. This is exactly the “at low scale, accuracy is noise, so find a smoother observable” move.
	**Pretraining choices → posttraining success, with pretrain performance held constant**
	This became a hot topic in 2025–2026:
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f812cb778cf35b640d7a8"/> (arXiv 2607.25063, 2026) — closest to your exact experimental design: models that look similar after SFT diverge under identical post-training depending on late-pretraining data interventions. It explicitly cites Dohare’s plasticity paper, so your two original threads have formally merged.
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816f9d14e85bc7839a54"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81fda8aeec4636caa4ec"/>, <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810e9cefc2deae8a54da"/> et al., COLM 2025; arXiv 2504.07912) — an end-to-end study that trains models from scratch on controlled pretraining mixtures, then compares PPO, GRPO, and Expert Iteration across scales. RL tends to amplify output patterns inherited from pretraining, while mixture- and scale-dependent settings include improvements, plateaus, and regressions. It also argues that controlled small-model proxies can yield real insight into RL behavior.
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8169920ed64ce7c0ddea"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815f9ddef6104ed3e9c9"/> et al., NVIDIA, ICLR 2026; arXiv 2510.03264) — in controlled 8B experiments, broad and diverse reasoning data is most useful during pretraining, while SFT benefits more from a smaller curated set of high-quality long-CoT examples. Large mixed-quality SFT datasets can dilute the advantages created by reasoning-aware pretraining.
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81bb8714ed6c14d9dd8c"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81b3ad30c4f2bfafb2f4"/> et al., arXiv 2605.12705, 2026) finds that moving some target-domain data into pretraining improves retention after subsequent fine-tuning, even when immediate post-training performance is similar. <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118156f783c9abdf89"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81e78c01d327cc5db046"/> et al., arXiv 2603.16177, 2026) supports the emerging view that early domain exposure can be more durable than introducing the same data only during fine-tuning, while the repetition schedule affects whether the model generalizes, overfits, or later forgets the domain.
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81f98cddfbf646091feb"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f819ebb85e87104b322ae"/> et al., arXiv 2607.16097, 2026) — lower pretraining loss strongly predicts higher post-RL pass@1 at fixed RL compute, while the local RL-improvement slope grows with log pretraining tokens. In a post-SFT auxiliary analysis, pretraining loss predicts large-k pass@k better than pass@1. This is in interesting tension with your original hypothesis because final pretraining loss carries strong predictive signal here, though not all of it.
	**Your “posttraining did nothing” wall — you were not alone**
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f818ca12ff951377df50b"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81f6b486e678d8cc0f33"/> et al., COLM 2025; arXiv 2504.07086) — RL applied to distillation-based models yields little to no statistically significant gain in the tested settings; some promising original reports do not hold up under repeated evaluation, and RL-trained base models still often underperform instruction-tuned counterparts. Small benchmarks produce unstable estimates, making multiple seed runs essential. This documents exactly the ‘hard to figure out from how people present their results’ phenomenon you ran into.
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810abfbfccb7341fea12"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/2846403706434f259c77b80b138c2f9c"/> et al., ICML 2026; arXiv 2506.10947) — on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, GRPO with random rewards improves MATH-500 by 21.4 points, nearly matching the gain from ground-truth rewards, while comparable spurious rewards generally fail outside Qwen families such as Llama 3.2 and OLMo 2. This supports the interpretation that some Qwen-centric RLVR gains reflect amplification of high-prior behaviors rather than generic reward learning.
	- <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f819590c0d8d6ff4067ac"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81a8b7d7f3c2d1572d0d"/> et al., NeurIPS 2025 oral; arXiv 2504.13837) — current RLVR methods often improve pass@k at small k but fail to expand the base model’s reasoning boundary at large k. <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81728d3ccf3ee573a1b9"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8122af36e57f1052e3a1"/> and <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f812f89b9fd46a28010f8"/>, AI for Math Workshop at ICML 2025) formalizes RLVR as predominantly support-preserving, entropy-reducing reweighting, while <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816f9d14e85bc7839a54"/> shows that post-training often amplifies patterns inherited from pretraining. The boundary is not absolute: <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81999d39ff7dce4f94d8"/> (arXiv 2507.14843) distinguishes nominal from empirical support and allows finite-budget expansion even though shrinkage usually dominates, and <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8147b6d3e47b454cbcaa"/> (arXiv 2506.14245; ICLR 2026) reports counter-results under CoT-Pass@K and executable pass@K.
	- On the small-model side specifically: <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f817e844bcd861034995a"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8107b32dc5a3941a37ad"/> et al., arXiv 2505.17988) shows that small-scale SFT on Qwen2.5-1.5B can reduce MATH-500 accuracy from 23.8% to 18.4% even while eliciting reasoning-style behaviors such as ‘Wait’ and ‘Let me check.’ <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815ebc24fad1ff1328ea"/> (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815fa8c8d996a773abfd"/> et al., EMNLP 2025; arXiv 2506.07712) studies Long CoT Degradation in small language models and attributes the effect to error accumulation.
	**Retrospective reading of your project**
	You were testing “pretraining shapes post-training beyond final loss” at scales where (a) post-training gains are largely elicitation of capabilities your models didn’t yet have, (b) the Qwen confound was silently inflating the literature’s baseline expectations, and (c) benchmark noise swamps effect sizes without multi-seed evaluation. All three of those are now papers. Your negative result was, in hindsight, an early observation of a real phenomenon — the field just hadn’t built the vocabulary for it yet.
	And the connective tissue back to your original question: the field is converging on exactly your framing — final pretraining loss is an insufficient statistic for downstream success, and the open question is what *else* about the training trajectory (data order, late-window exposure, the “state of the learner” in plasticity terms) predicts post-training outcomes.

<summary>**Alternatives to Our Post-Training Experiment Design**</summary>
	> *2026-08-18 13:49:09 *I was skeptical of returning to the post-training project because:
		- Iteration is so slow.
		- You really do need more seeds to see real differences.
		- Everything is so model specific that you’d need a fairly large sweep just to debug a small scale issue. 
		- Limiting yourself to existing “clean” pretraining sweeps makes it near impossible to test for model family effects. 
		- The data decide models are all tiny and have the “no movement during SFT” issue we started with.
		At the same time, I don’t want to fully abandon the whole direction. 
	**The Tension:** The noise floor of your measurements scales with the number of seeds you can afford, while the generality of any finding scales with the number of model families you test — and both multiply against slow iteration. These also interact: a clean single-family result might just be another family artifact, so even a successful sweep has uncertain external validity.
	**Consider a different question. **“Why can’t anyone measure whether X improves Y at affordable scale?” The measurement-and-proxy angle is both the least blocked and, right now, probably the most needed.
	**Make the proxy metric the contribution, not the sweep.** DataDecide’s insight (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) — continuous likelihood beats accuracy at small scale — hasn’t really been transplanted to post-training. A continuous, low-variance predictor of “RL-ability” measured on the base model (NLL on gold reasoning traces, [pass@k](mailto:pass@k) at large k, entropy at decision points, even plasticity-style statistics like curvature or feature rank) would let *everyone else* escape the seed problem. Validating a proxy needs far fewer runs than detecting an intervention effect, because you’re fitting a correlation across existing variation rather than powering a comparison.
	**Turn the blocker into the object of study.** The Sober Look paper (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f818ca12ff951377df50b"/>) did variance analysis for evaluation; nobody has done the equivalent power analysis for post-training *experiments* — how many seeds does a claimed RLVR delta actually require at 150M vs 1B, and how much of the published small-scale literature clears that bar? That’s mostly re-analysis of public results plus a modest number of your own runs, and it directly legitimizes your negative result.
	**Fully synthetic testbeds.** The Echo Chamber (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816f9d14e85bc7839a54"/>) and graph-pathfinding style of work (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815b89e1d2dbfb965bc1"/>) shows you can study pretraining-conditioned post-training mechanistically in settings where a seed costs minutes. Findings are less directly transferable, but they’re *causal*, and the field currently has correlational LLM results and toy theory with little in between.
	**Interrogate whether “no movement” was real or a metric artifact.** This is the one I’d push hardest. “SFT did nothing” almost certainly means benchmark accuracy didn’t move — but did the model move in distribution space? NLL on held-out reasoning traces, KL from the base model, calibration, sample diversity, [pass@k](mailto:pass@k) at very large k are all continuous and much lower-variance than accuracy. Two possibilities, both publishable: either the models genuinely don’t move even in likelihood space (a stronger, stranger negative result than “accuracy flat”), or they *do* move and pretraining recipes differ in *how much* — in which case your original question is answerable at DataDecide scale after all, with the accuracy threshold reframed as the thing that was hiding it. This is the same trick DataDecide itself (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) used to make MMLU predictable at 150M, just applied one stage later.
	**Lower the task instead of raising the model.** The “no movement” is a property of the model–task pair, not the model. TinyZero-style results (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81209571f76cdaac1ea9"/>) show RL visibly works at 0.5–3B on countdown and simple arithmetic; you can design verifiable tasks whose difficulty sits just above the base models’ zero-shot ability. Then recipe effects on post-training become measurable at sweepable scale. The open question this creates — do recipe effects on within-reach tasks predict recipe effects on out-of-reach tasks at larger scale? — is itself a gap nobody has addressed, and it only needs a couple of larger validation runs, not a factorial sweep.
	**Get family diversity from the last window, not from scratch.** You can’t pretrain five families, but you can take OLMo, Pythia, SmolLM, Llama, and Qwen checkpoints and apply controlled *late-window* continued pretraining — same intervention, same tokens, different lineages. The Final Window paper’s claim (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f812cb778cf35b640d7a8"/>) is that this window disproportionately shapes post-training behavior, which if true means most of the family-effect question is testable at annealing cost rather than pretraining cost. If the claim is false at your scales, that’s also a finding.
	The asymmetric design ties these together: 
	- Full sweep with seeds only where it’s cheap (small models, continuous metrics, easy tasks).
	- Then spend the expensive budget on two or three confirmation runs testing a *ranking* the cheap tier predicted — a much lower-powered, therefore affordable, test than estimating effect sizes.

<summary>**Use In-Context Learning as the Post-Training<br>  **→ “ICL-ability as a cheap predictor of finetunability across pretraining recipes.”<br>  → “How compressible code is into natural language *for a given model pair* is a property of their <br>        shared representations, so reconstruction fidelity could itself serve as a capability probe, one <br>        that’s graded rather than thresholded, unlike [pass@1](mailto:pass@1).”</summary>
	>
		*2026-08-18 13:56:06* Alternatively could we treating “in context learning” as the “post-training” and explore any features that could be extracted from that or elicitation.
	**ICL as the post-training stage.** This directly attacks your two blockers. 
	- Iteration: an ICL “training run” costs one forward pass, so seeds become cheap — you can average over prompt orderings and samples the way you never could over SFT runs. 
	- Elicitation threshold: there’s a real line of work arguing ICL and fine-tuning are two access routes to the same latent capabilities — e.g., a formal argument that capabilities acquired through SFT can be approximated by the base model via in-context learning without parameter updates (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8132a9efc21990046edb"/>), and empirical comparisons of when each paradigm wins: prompt-based methods excel in few-shot scenarios but their effectiveness plateaus as more data becomes available (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f817887a9e59e3fe2fb2b"/>). 
		- That last paper (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f817887a9e59e3fe2fb2b"/>) is a fun full-circle moment for you: it’s by Bornschein, <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c2de135cd1f80f9a315ff7bbdf9894e"/> , <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/6caaec61b10a4118ad42d61049453e12"/> et al. — the plasticity crowd literally moved into “ICL vs fine-tuning as adaptation” territory. Your instinct is convergent with where that sub-community went.
	**Nobody has validated ICL-ability as a cheap predictor of finetunability across pretraining recipes. **
	- Take your existing DataDecide-style checkpoints (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>)
	- Measure in-context learning curves (loss on the k-th demo as a function of k — a continuous, smooth, per-token metric, no benchmark thresholds)
	- Ask whether recipe differences show up there even where SFT accuracy was flat. 
	- If ICL slope at 150M–1B predicts post-training movement at larger scale, you’ve built exactly the proxy metric the field is missing, using the models you already have. 
	- And if models differ in ICL-ability while being identical in final loss, that’s your original thesis — pretraining shapes the *learner*, not just the *snapshot* — demonstrated without a single gradient update.
	**The code autoencoder.** 
	- Mechanically, prompt optimization *is* elicitation pushed to its ceiling — you’re measuring what a frozen model can be made to do, which is the same quantity the ICL idea probes, just optimized rather than sampled. 
	- Conceptually, your reconstruction loss is another instance of your recurring move: a continuous, cheap, differentiable-ish signal (round-trip fidelity through the natural-language bottleneck) standing in for a fuzzy capability (“does the model *understand* this code”). 
	- The bottleneck itself is interesting for the pretraining question too: **how compressible code is into natural language *for a given model pair* is a property of their shared representations**, so reconstruction fidelity could itself serve as a capability probe — one that’s graded rather than thresholded, unlike [pass@1](mailto:pass@1).
	**Link between the two: **the autoencoder’s encoder/decoder prompts are “trained” parameters living in text space, so you’re doing gradient-free adaptation where a “seed” is a sampling temperature draw. Your whole trajectory — loss curves, proxy metrics, elicitation — keeps circling one question: *what cheap continuous observable reveals latent capability?*

<summary>**How to Combine Vision and Language for the ICL/Post-training Experiments**</summary>
	>
		*2026-08-18 14:35:09* I started out looking at plasticity in earlier CNN stuff, and then looking at what results I could get confidence bounds for and which I couldn’t. Despite not finishing that direction, I think there’s value to having both CNN experiments at a small scale in vision and LLM experiments in the larger scale for NLP.  I’m wondering if there’s a way to stay in this realm of experiments and combine these two spaces, potentially incorporating vision transformers instead of CNNs for clear comparisons if it would help.
	**Pretraining Choices Shape Elicitability, Holding Final Performance Constant. **This doesn’t require language at all. ICL is the perfect “post-training” stand-in because it’s gradient-free, continuous (per-token loss on the k-th in-context example), and cheap enough to run with the many seeds your CNN experience taught you that you need for confidence bounds.
	- Chan et al. (2022, <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814f827bd8a3c57b7e60"/>) showed with small transformers on Omniglot-style image sequences that whether ICL emerges *at all* depends on pretraining data properties (burstiness, class distribution skew, within-class variation) — often with little difference in ordinary training loss. Raventós et al. did the analogous thing for linear-regression ICL, finding a task-diversity threshold (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8193a19cd317cae2b333"/>). That literature is your hypothesis, already demonstrated in miniature — but it’s framed as “when does ICL emerge,” not as “ICL-ability as a measurable functional of pretraining recipe that predicts adaptation at larger scale.” That reframing is your gap.
	**Proposed Sequence**
	1. **Tiny-transformer vision/synthetic tier (weeks, full statistical rigor).** Adapt your CNN pipeline to small ViTs or sequence-transformers on Omniglot/CIFAR-style ICL tasks à la Chan (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814f827bd8a3c57b7e60"/>). Vary pretraining recipe (data distribution properties, ordering, mixture), train many seeds, and — critically — construct **matched-loss checkpoint pairs**: select checkpoints across recipes at equal pretraining loss, then measure ICL curves. This is the clean version of your dead project: same question, but the outcome variable moves, and you can afford the seeds.
	2. **Define the invariant measurement, not the invariant model.** The bridge between tiers is a protocol: ICL curve slope/asymptote at matched loss, with a power analysis (your old confidence-bound work is directly reusable here — it becomes methodology, not a detour). The deliverable of tier 1 is “here is a low-variance elicitability metric and here’s how many seeds it needs.”
	3. **DataDecide tier (one replication, not a sweep).** Reproduce your pipeline on a handful of DataDecide recipes (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) and measure the *same* protocol — ICL loss curves on held-out tasks, matched-loss comparisons. <summary>**Analyzing ICL**</summary>
	>
		*2026-08-18 15:45:13* How you can analyze what’s happening during in context learning? Are there gradient approximations? What types of statistics have people come up with to measure the intermediate impact of ICL?
	**The “gradient approximation” idea is real and has a whole literature — plus an important debunking arc.** 
	- The founding results: von Oswald et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81209231f16a83684256"/>) showed you can construct key/query/value matrices such that one linear self-attention step on the tokens is *identical* to gradient-induced dynamics — the forward pass literally implements a GD step in a simplified setting. 
	- Akyürek et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81e380d1e9e13a6d1992"/>) extended this to ridge regression
	- Dai et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f812d9458c32c7a692e69"/>) drew the connection through a kernel perspective and framed ICL as implicit finetuning.  This is the most directly usable for you: they define concrete similarity statistics between attention-induced hidden-state updates and actual fine-tuning gradient updates.  But based on the contradictory findings, use the GD-similarity statistics, but with the untrained-model control the original papers skipped.
	- Ahn et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81f7a2a7d2e0ecdde653"/>) showed transformers learn *preconditioned* gradient descent. 
	- **Contradiction**: A revisit on realistic NLP tasks found gaps in the evaluation — problematic metrics, insufficient baselines, and, damningly, even *untrained* models achieve comparable ICL–GD similarity scores despite not exhibiting ICL. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81219228f20f2fe8d677"/>) 
	- **Contradiction**: And newer theory shows whether ICL corresponds to gradient descent at all depends on precise conditions (e.g., initialization), identified in tractable linear-self-attention settings. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81edac78c73dee3b0aac"/>)
	**The extractable-object family: task/function/state vectors.** Instead of approximating a gradient, extract the “update” itself as an activation-space object. 
	- Hendel et al.’s task vectors (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8139a90ac3dc4859d865"/>) and 
	- Todd et al.’s function vectors (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81a3bfa8d44f5d391b9a"/>) show a demonstration set gets compressed into a transportable hidden-state vector; 
	- follow-ups formalize a “state vector” capturing the ICL processing state stored in attention activations, explicitly analyzing its similarities with parameters trained via gradient descent, and even apply optimizer-style tricks (inner and momentum optimization) (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f819dba6aee11b01e682c"/>) to refine it. 
	- More recent work represents ICL tasks as a learned weighted sum of all attention heads. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81a99203f77a351429ec"/>) 
	These give you parameter-like measurables without parameters: vector norm (how much “learning” happened), direction stability across demo orderings (cheap variance estimates), transferability across prompts (generalization), and — the recipe-comparison payoff — whether two base models pretrained differently produce differently-structured task vectors at matched loss.
	**The statistics I’d build your protocol on, in rough order of robustness:**
	1. *In-context loss curves* — per-token loss as a function of context position / number of demonstrations. Olsson et al.’s original “ICL score” (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81939822f622f7239dc5"/>) was literally the loss difference between an early and late token position. Smooth, continuous, seed-cheap — the direct descendant of your loss-curve-features thesis.
	2. *Induction-head strength* (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81939822f622f7239dc5"/>) (prefix-matching/copying scores on synthetic sequences) — the mechanistic correlate of ICL emergence, measurable per-checkpoint, so you can watch elicitability *develop* along the pretraining trajectory.
	3. *Task-recognition vs. task-learning decomposition* (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8187bdbdedc52c88aacf"/>) — shuffled-label and format-only controls separate “the demos told the model which task” from “the model learned the mapping.” Matters because pretraining recipes plausibly affect these two components differently.
	4. *Task-vector geometry*, as above.
	5. *ICL–GD similarity*, last, and only with the adversarial controls.
	And the satisfying convergence: the field’s best ICL measurement is a loss curve over context position, fit with power laws, complete with emergence thresholds and proxy-metric debates — your pretraining-scale question recapitulated inside a single forward pass.

<summary>**Loss Basins and the River-Valley Explanation<br>   **→ “Treat basin membership as a covariate of proxy-metric validity: <br>            Test whether ‘Metrics are comparable iff linear mode connectivity (two models are in the same basin)’.<br>            Report your elicitability comparisons *conditional on* barrier height. <br>            If recipe effects on ICL-ability only hold within low-barrier pairs, that’s a finding.<br>            If they hold across basins, that’s a stronger one.”</summary>
	>
		*2026-08-18 16:44:32 *Another related idea is that of the loss basins and the river valley explanation of training.  My understanding is that these metrics are sometimes only relevant/comparable between models if they’re in the same basin.  Is there currently a way to identify whether a model is currently in the valley versus climbing the mountains? And is there a way to identify whether two models are in the same basin?
	**This Topic is Important to Your Experimental Design.** You’re comparing observables (ICL curves, task vectors, plasticity statistics) across models at matched loss.  Loss is a scalar projection of position in parameter space, where two models with equal loss can sit in different valleys or in the same valley but at different points along the river vs. up the wall. The basin question is therefore the question of *when your comparisons are well-defined*.
	1. **The multi-power law paper itself flags this.** (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/29ba77d7fdf949e1a1f9bdb4df8c35a4"/>) Its authors note the river-valley conjecture as the landscape framework their schedule-dependent loss terms are implicitly modeling. The “decay-induced loss drop” term is, in river-valley language, descending from oscillating on the walls down to the river: during the stable phase the iterate oscillates due to the high learning rate yet progresses swiftly along the river; during decay, shrinking oscillations move it closer to the river, revealing true optimization progress. So “loss” during training conflates along-river progress (durable) with distance-from-river (transient) — exactly the two components your matched-loss design needs to distinguish. Two recipes matched on loss could be matched on totally different mixes of the two.
	2. **Warm-starting is a basin story too.** Initializing from a converged solution means starting deep in a basin shaped by the small dataset — and the ELR-re-warming result (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81998a6efd31b1a5c05b"/>) (that re-warming closes the gap) is naturally read as “you need enough effective temperature to leave/reshape the basin.”
	3. **The comparability problem has a sharp precedent. **Juneja et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81f1b9dbc417bb11a746"/>) (ICLR 2023) showed that fine-tuned models cluster into distinct linearly-connected basins, and models in *different* basins implement *different generalization strategies* (e.g., heuristic vs. syntax-sensitive on NLI) despite similar in-distribution accuracy. That’s the strongest existing evidence for your suspicion: functionally, “same metric value, different basin” can mean different mechanisms, so mechanism-level metrics (task vectors, GD-similarity scores) may not be comparable across basins at all.
	**There’s no settled scalar measure of “same basin” or “on the river.”** What exists is a toolkit of pairwise tests: interpolation barrier (raw and permutation-aligned), the convex-vs-monotone interpolation signature, feature-space connectivity, curve-collapse — each partial. 
	**Nobody has connected either literature to *metric validity*: no paper says “ICL scores / task vectors / plasticity statistics are comparable iff models pass test X.” **For your design this suggests a cheap, high-value addition: log the pairwise interpolation barrier (with and without alignment) between every pair of checkpoints you compare, and report your elicitability comparisons *conditional on* barrier height. If recipe effects on ICL-ability only hold within low-barrier pairs, that’s a finding; if they hold across basins, that’s a stronger one. 
	**Recent Work on Valley/Mountain Position Measurement**
	- Wen et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e"/>) (arXiv 2410.05192) is the canonical statement
		- pretraining loss conjectured to resemble a deep valley with a river at its bottom; the sustained high-LR phase drives progress along the river and the decay phase drives progress in the mountain direction, both critical. 
		- Their proposed *measurement* is interpolation-based: the loss on the linear interpolation between two stable-phase checkpoints is convex and unimodal (a valley cross-section), while between two decay-phase checkpoints it decays smoothly and monotonically. 
		- That interpolation signature is currently the closest thing to a “river test.” 
		- They also offer a mechanism for *why* language loss has this shape: highly deterministic tokens (facts, knowledge) contribute the river direction, while uncertain, ambiguous tokens create the steep hillsides — which is interesting for you because it says the valley geometry is *data-property-dependent*, i.e., plausibly recipe-dependent. 
	- Follow-up work has started visualizing it directly (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f815f9794dce512240a97"/>): plotting the landscape in coordinates defined by the global pre-cooldown→final direction vs. the local Adam-steps direction, explicitly noting that a clear visualization of the river valley had been lacking.
	- The “Scaling with Collapse” line (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8156af2aebd75a3acd83"/>) (arXiv 2509.25087) pushes the practical version: well-tuned training runs’ loss curves collapse onto a shared shape, suggesting families of runs traveling the same river — a possible cross-run comparability criterion built from curves alone, no weight access needed.
	**Recent Work on Basin Identification**
	- The operational definition remains linear mode connectivity — interpolate two weight sets; if performance along the path stays comparable to the endpoints, they probably lie in the same loss basin. 
	- Same-run-early-split models are linearly connected (Frankle et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810ab141d9246c14a861"/>))
	- Independently trained models are connected *only after permutation alignment* (Entezari conjecture (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810a827ed0e88fc0a75b"/>), Git Re-Basin (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81e4aa5acb3bacac84e2"/>)), though re-basin methods often reduce barriers only marginally and work poorly early in training, with no unified theory of when they succeed. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81cb81c2fe9da0d72c58"/>) 
	- Layerwise linear *feature* connectivity (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81219422c244e516c484"/>) (connectivity in activation space, not just loss), and cross-task linearity in the pretraining–finetuning paradigm (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f812386b7e56fafe23cc6"/>) — models fine-tuned from a common checkpoint stay in a shared linear regime, which is precisely why task arithmetic (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81b89537cc8695643550"/>) and model souping (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81919bf2fd4fbc6343c7"/>) work, and why they *only* work within a basin. 
	- The 2026 theory frontier is trying to explain basin structure via neuron identifiability (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8111a999f70dba836177"/>) — whether features get consistently assigned to neurons across random seeds — which would, if it matures, give a principled answer to “when are two models’ internal metrics comparable.”

<summary>*2026-08-18 20:12:27 *ok, can you lay out the Causal Rep Learning related work and concepts that form the foundation of your proposed approach to tying the 3 foundation paper grid into identifiability?</summary>
	Here’s the foundation, organized from formalism to operational measurements — with honest notes on which links are established and which are the analogical leaps your project would be making explicit.
		**1. The core formalism: identifiability as “unique up to a symmetry group.”** The template result-shape comes from nonlinear ICA: Hyvärinen & Pajunen (1999) (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81fbabdaf5e81af3df79"/>) proved that latent variables behind observed data are *fundamentally non-identifiable* from observational i.i.d. data alone — infinitely many incompatible latent structures produce the same distribution. The modern era (Hyvärinen’s time-contrastive (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81c49f32dd86a3bf3a19"/>) and auxiliary-variable work (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81c3bc91df12aa6e1d80"/>); Khemakhem et al.’s iVAE, 2020 (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8162a195dbc75c8220fb"/>)) showed identifiability is *restored* by additional structure: auxiliary variables, non-stationarity, or multiple environments, with recovery guaranteed only up to a residual group (permutations, elementwise transforms). This gives you the grammar for everything else: a learning problem’s solution set is characterized by (data, objective, function class) up to a group G; anything not pinned down by those is available for the *trajectory* to select. “Path dependence exists” = “the residual underdetermination beyond G is nonempty and SGD’s selection within it is history-sensitive.” That’s the translation that turns your grid’s “permanent damage” results into statements about solution-class selection.
		**2. Interventions buy identifiability — the CRL results that license your methodology.** Causal representation learning (Schölkopf et al., “Toward Causal Representation Learning,” 2021 (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8139a00ac80ed1f18dc9"/>)) inherits the ICA impossibility and its cure: observational data underdetermines latent causal factors, but *interventional* data identifies them. The concrete results: Brehmer et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810ea1ddeb8612cc15d0"/>) (2022) prove identifiability of causal representations from paired pre/post-intervention observations; Ahuja et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8191a9c9d1edc80851e2"/>) give identifiability from interventions under geometric assumptions; von Kügelgen et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810c93fdd1acaab66f9c"/>) (2021) show contrastive augmentation-pairs provably isolate content from style; and the *sparse mechanism shift* principle (distribution shifts tend to change few mechanisms) makes shifts themselves identification signal. The analogical move for your project — and it’s an analogy in structure, not a theorem you can import — is one level up: treat *the training process* as the system, observational access = loss/eval curves, and your deficit windows, warm-starts, decay branches, and distillation arms as the interventions that identify what curves cannot.
