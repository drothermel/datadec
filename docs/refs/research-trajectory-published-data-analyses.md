		*2026-08-18 17:03:03* I feel like the DataDecide (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) models would be more useful if they were trained with WSD learning rate schedule because then we could actually do comparable annealing tests along the course of the trajectory.  Unfortunately their evals are actually just showing the un-annealed or partially annealed performance directly after pretraining.  This skews all results including post-training performance.
		Then, what are some of our options to work around this problem? Are there ways to do small extensions of the training from each checkpoint to gather more data? Or, would redo-ing some portion of the pretraining using WSD enable some interesting experiments? How does related work interact with this question.
		Separately, how does the field currently quantify differences between datasets? They become approximately black boxes because they are SO large, but we analyze trained models which actually are black boxes, so there must be things we can do for datasets as well.
	**The annealing confound.** You’re right about the mechanics: DataDecide (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>)’s models were trained OLMo-style (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8124879cffe1021cc330"/>) with cosine schedules, so every intermediate checkpoint sits mid-schedule with high residual LR — in river-valley terms, evals on those checkpoints measure “position along river + current distance up the wall,” and the wall component is schedule-dependent noise relative to the question you care about. Post-training from such checkpoints inherits the confound (you’re fine-tuning from a point high on the wall). The relevant related work:
	- **Hägele et al. 2024** (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811da333f746a38f3684"/>) made essentially your argument as a methodology proposal: constant LR + short cooldown matches cosine, so scaling-law and data experiments should use stable-phase runs with cheap decay branches instead of retraining full cosine runs per budget. 
	- The stable-checkpoint-plus-1-sqrt-decay-resume protocol is now standard in careful small-scale studies, and MiniCPM (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81d3bad5cbd562773738"/>) established the practical template — a stable-phase checkpoint plus a fixed-length decay matches full cosine baselines, with \~10% decay completing convergence and new data mixed in strictly during decay. 
	- Frontier labs use exactly this for data valuation (Llama 3 (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81d8b591f5797b403be0"/>)’s annealing-based data-quality assessments; Blakeney et al.’s “Does your data spark joy?” (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81ce81dcc7287955be73"/>)). So “annealing branches as the correct eval” is validated practice — but no *open, multi-recipe suite* has it. 
	- **DataDecide-with-WSD-branches** (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>)** is a genuine gap**, and at 150M–300M scales redoing a subset of recipes is affordable; you’d be producing the dataset the field’s own methodology papers say should exist.
	**Two cheap partial fixes on the *existing* checkpoints**. 
	- **Checkpoint merging as pseudo-annealing**
		- Merging recent checkpoints (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816abd01d38ba73e1c77"/>) with weights derived from an emulated decay curve provides a robust annealed model without ever altering the live learning rate
		- WSM-merged models (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816abd01d38ba73e1c77"/>) consistently and closely mirror the results of a true LR anneal at intermediate stages of long runs. 
		- Production models now use this as their mid-run readout: Nemotron 3 (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f817db04de2282ca800d8"/>) applies sliding-window checkpoint merging to get stronger quality readouts without dedicated decay runs, estimating savings of \~16% of total pretraining FLOPs. 
		- **→ The open question — whether merging-as-annealing-proxy works on *cosine* mid-run checkpoints (varying LR within the merge window) rather than stable-phase ones — is itself a nice small experiment, and if it works even approximately you can retrofit “annealed” evals onto all of DataDecide for the cost of evals. **
	- **The multi-power law** (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/29ba77d7fdf949e1a1f9bdb4df8c35a4"/>)** predicts the loss drop from a hypothetical decay given the trajectory so far, so you could correct unannealed loss curves analytically.** 
		- It won’t give you downstream metrics, but it quantifies how much each recipe’s apparent ranking is schedule artifact. 
		- One caveat to your framing: DataDecide (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) found intermediate-checkpoint *decisions* matched compute-equivalent final checkpoints — so the confound may partially cancel for rankings even while distorting levels and post-training. 
		- Testing when it cancels vs. doesn’t is exactly what your WSD branches would settle. 
		- Also note the river-valley theory (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e"/>) predicts the decay phase itself makes progress along the river — so annealed evals aren’t a pure “reveal” either; branch length becomes a parameter to control.
	**Dataset featurization.** Your framing — datasets aren’t black boxes, just obscured by size — matches where the field is, and the current toolkit splits into three families:
	1. **Model-mediated features** (dominant, works best, philosophically unsatisfying for you): describe data by how models respond to it. Perplexity correlations (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81eda703dfcb3298681d"/>) uses losses of 90 public LLMs on tens of thousands of web domains as features, exploiting the observation that losses on pretraining texts correlate with downstream benchmark performance; RegMix (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81289b7ed3c13fb9f346"/>) formulates mixture selection as a regression task from small proxy runs, matching or beating DoReMi (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81ad971eea97f46d750e"/>) with \~10% of the compute; and data mixing laws (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8184a396c885d7adc8b7"/>) (BiMix (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814e8a35e7e86ba02efa"/>) etc.) fit parametric laws jointly over domain proportion and data volume. These predict outcomes well but featurize the dataset as “mixture weights over named domains” — they don’t tell you *what property* of the data mattered.
	2. **Dataset-similarity embeddings**: Task2Vec-based (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811694d9c6ff7b5946e6"/>) alignment coefficients (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f810b8e84d0c64846d5a5"/>) quantify similarity between two datasets and, in controlled interventional experiments, measure alignment’s impact on downstream performance. Related: the diversity coefficient (Miranda et al.) (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81039425cc7740bd7220"/>) — expected Task2Vec distance between batches — which correlates with downstream quality, and FID-style dataset distances in other modalities. Important negative result to respect: data similarity alone is not enough to explain language model performance (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81a3ab0de58e2a85ea63"/>).
	3. **Intrinsic corpus statistics** (closest to your instinct, least developed): WIMBD (Elazar et al.) (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8155bd6ee458dbcf713c"/>) computes corpus-level statistics — duplication, contamination, domain composition, length distributions — at trillion-token scale, proving the “obscured by size” problem is tractable; compression-based measures (gzip ratio, entropy-law style) as scalar complexity/redundancy features; Zipf/burstiness/type-token statistics — which, recall, are exactly the properties Chan et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814f827bd8a3c57b7e60"/>) showed *cause* ICL emergence in small transformers. That last link matters: the one place intrinsic data statistics have been causally tied to a capability is your Chapter-2 setting.
	**Open Question: Intrinsic Features Impact on Controlled Outcomes.**
	- DataDecide (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) hands you a supervised problem — 25 corpora with measured outcomes (and \~300 pairwise decisions, plus per-task breakdowns).
	- No one has systematically featurized those 25 corpora (WIMBD statistics (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8155bd6ee458dbcf713c"/>), diversity coefficient (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81039425cc7740bd7220"/>), compression ratios, perplexity-correlation profiles (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81eda703dfcb3298681d"/>)) and asked which features predict the outcome table, or whether intrinsic features match model-mediated ones.
	**Open Question: Does a dataset’s “determinism profile” predict landscape geometry?**
	- The river-valley hypothesis (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811dbaf3d4ffdd782a5e"/>) **attributes the valley geometry itself to data properties** — **deterministic tokens forming the river**, **uncertain tokens the walls** — meaning a dataset’s “determinism profile” (cheap to estimate with any reference model) is a candidate feature predicting not just performance but *landscape geometry*, i.e., annealing behavior. 
	- That would tie your WSD-branch suite and your featurization question into one design: features of the data predicting both where the river goes and how much the decay reveals.

<summary>*2026-08-18 22:34:55 *I’m actually interested in starting with looking for changes between the provided pretrain checkpoints. and please reference the signal vs noise paper when answering.</summary>
	Perfect paper to anchor this, because your proposed project is literally its dual. Heineman et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>) define signal as a benchmark’s ability to separate better models from worse models, and noise as a benchmark’s sensitivity to random variability between training steps — i.e., in their framework, *checkpoint-to-checkpoint movement is the nuisance term*, something to be suppressed so that model-level comparisons stabilize. Your project inverts the roles: treat inter-checkpoint change as the *object*, and ask what structure lives inside what they averaged away as noise. Same data, opposite marginal. And critically, the data already exists: they released 900K evaluation results on 465 open-weight models, including evaluations across intermediate checkpoints of OLMo (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8124879cffe1021cc330"/>), the DataDecide suite (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>), and the ladder models (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/a46c70a5768e4964b656aeb958b2ed8a"/>). So Stage 0 of your project is pure analysis of a published artifact — no inference, no GPUs, start today.
		Here’s how I’d structure it:
		**Stage 0 (their data, zero compute): decompose their “noise” into drift and diffusion.** “Variability between training steps” conflates two processes with opposite meanings: stochastic jitter (the checkpoint oscillating on the valley walls — order-free, mean-reverting) and genuine learning (progress along the river — directional, cumulative). These are separable from a trajectory alone: autocorrelation structure, sign-consistency of increments, variance growth with lag (diffusion grows linearly in lag; drift grows quadratically). Run that decomposition per benchmark × per recipe × per scale over their released trajectories, and you get the movement version of their SNR table: which evals detect *learning between adjacent checkpoints* vs. which only wiggle. Immediate payoffs: their own intervention findings (perplexity-type metrics beat accuracy on signal and noise; filtering noisy subtasks improves aggregate reliability) get re-derived and sharpened — you’d predict continuous metrics have high drift-to-diffusion ratio, accuracy metrics low — and any benchmark where *recipes differ in drift/diffusion signature at matched loss* is your thesis phenomenon appearing in data someone else already published. There’s also a direct river-valley test hiding here: diffusion magnitude should scale with the current LR (walls), drift should not (river), and the cosine schedule’s gradual decay gives you a natural LR sweep *within* each trajectory to check it.
		**Stage 1 (cheap inference): richer movement metrics between adjacent checkpoints.** Their released results are benchmark-level; the mechanistically interesting movement is finer. For a subset of recipes and checkpoint pairs: per-token KL(checkpoint_t ‖ checkpoint_t+1) on a fixed probe corpus, prediction-flip rates on benchmark items (the churn literature’s measure — what fraction of “noise” is the same marginal accuracy hiding large item-level exchange), and layerwise representation drift (CKA (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f818c9d77cd8138f86848"/>) / linear-map residual per layer). Then slice per-token KL by the entropy buckets from our earlier discussion: the concrete hypothesis is that adjacent-checkpoint movement concentrates on high-entropy (hillside) tokens mid-schedule — i.e., their “noise” is not uniformly distributed over the data distribution but is *the walls, localized to the stochastic tokens*, while low-entropy tokens carry the drift. If that holds, you’ve connected the Signal-and-Noise (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>) engineering framework to the landscape mechanism in one figure, and you get a practical dividend they’d care about: a principled recipe for constructing low-noise evals (weight items by token-determinism) rather than their empirical subtask filtering.
		**Stage 2: separate transient from durable movement causally.** The drift/diffusion decomposition is statistical; the causal version uses the tricks we’ve discussed. Compare KL(t, t+k) as a function of k (transient movement cancels; durable movement accumulates), and — the stronger instrument — apply checkpoint merging (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816abd01d38ba73e1c77"/>) or a short decay branch at t and t+k and compare the *annealed* models: movement that survives annealing is river movement by construction. That gives you an operational definition the field currently lacks: **durable movement = change that persists under the schedule-neutralizing transform**, with the Signal-and-Noise (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>) “noise” then decomposing into (measurement noise) + (wall oscillation) + (drift you’re too coarse to see), each separately estimated.
		Two design notes. First, the checkpoint spacing in the released suites sets your temporal resolution — DataDecide checkpoints (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) are relatively sparse, so adjacent-pair statistics at small k may be dominated by drift simply because thousands of steps separate saves; the OLMo trajectories (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8124879cffe1021cc330"/>) in the same release are denser and better for the diffusion estimates, then transfer the fitted decomposition to DataDecide’s sparser grid. Second, this project has an unusually clean relationship to your constraints: Stage 0 is a defensible artifact in weeks (an analysis paper on public data, exactly the “movement microscope” instrument-building framing), it name-checks and extends a NeurIPS 2025 framework from the group whose suite you depend on — which is also, not incidentally, the natural collaboration hook with Ai2 if the independent path ever activates — and every stage feeds the later program: the drift/diffusion fits are the noise floor for any future post-training movement claim, and the durable-movement operator is the instrument your matched-loss comparisons have needed all along.

<summary>*2026-08-18 22:37:47 *You can train moe models that do something at 20-50M active params, so I’m interested in looking all the way down to that scale.</summary>
	Going down to 20–50M active via MoE is a better move for your program than it might first appear, because MoE doesn’t just make tiny models capable — it adds a *new observable channel* that’s almost custom-built for your movement microscope. And the infrastructure situation is unusually good: FLAME-MoE (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81c38d82d8bd8f4ad47f"/>) is essentially DataDecide-for-MoE (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) at exactly your target scale — seven decoder-only MoE models from 38M to 1.7B active parameters, 64 experts per layer, top-8 gating, with full openness: code, data, checkpoints, routing logs, and evaluation results — and their training traces already show expert specialization emerging early and intensifying, co-activation staying sparse and stable, and routing behaviors converging quickly during early pretraining. Released routing logs across checkpoints means your Stage-0 (zero-training analysis of public trajectories) extends to MoE immediately.
		What routing buys each piece of your program:
		**For the movement microscope: a discrete, high-sensitivity channel.** Dense-model movement between checkpoints is continuous drift you have to dig out of KL and CKA; MoE gives you *categorical* movement — per-token expert-assignment flips — which is exactly the kind of high-signal observable the Signal-and-Noise (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>) logic favors. The field has already built the metric vocabulary: router saturation (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81979876da45b43ac416"/>) is defined as the average overlap between the top-k experts selected per token at step t versus at convergence, rising sharply within the first few thousand steps, with deeper layers saturating faster than shallower ones. Your drift/diffusion decomposition applies directly: routing flips that revert are wall oscillation, flips that persist are river movement, and saturation curves are literally cumulative-commitment plots. Slice by your token-entropy buckets and you get a sharp question nobody has asked: do hillside tokens keep flipping experts long after river tokens’ routes have frozen?
		**For the commitment/critical-period story: routing is a visible basin choice.** In dense models, “solution-class selection” is something you infer from interpolation barriers; in MoE, one large piece of it is *directly observable* — which expert claims which token family is a discrete, symmetric, underdetermined choice that gets made early and sticks. The evidence is already there in fragments: load-balance dynamics move through three phases (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f816288ecfc92aeff395c"/>) — an early balance-prioritizing phase, a stabilization phase where experts specialize, and a late relaxation phase trading balance for quality — a non-monotone trajectory invisible to post-hoc analysis of converged models, with annealing checkpoints confirming the phases are pretraining-specific and stable during fine-tuning. That “stable during fine-tuning” clause is a commitment claim in your exact sense. And the warm-starting cell of your grid has an MoE-native precedent to build on: under continual pretraining (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814381aedabff8740a50"/>), routing decisions change most in early layers, with the no-replay condition showing the most dramatic early-layer routing reorganization and the most forgetting — suggesting early-layer routing changes may be a key mechanism of catastrophic forgetting in MoEs. So router-saturation timestamps join Fisher trace, LLC, and LMC onset as a fourth commitment clock in your money figure — with the advantage of being the cheapest to compute and the only one that’s exactly zero/one per token.
		**For identifiability: MoE is simultaneously the best testbed and a warning.** Best testbed, because expert assignment is a textbook non-identifiable latent: the objective is invariant to expert permutation, so *which* expert specializes in what is pure trajectory-selected symmetry breaking — your CRL framing made concrete. And there’s already a striking empirical datapoint: specialization patterns in pretrained MoEs resist human interpretation (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81938aebe8a90924e218"/>), with expert overlap between different models answering the same question no higher than between entirely different questions — i.e., independently trained MoEs select *unrelated* specialization solutions, which is about as clean an existence proof of solution-class underdetermination as the field has produced. The same paper supplies the mechanistic reading you’d want: routers are linear maps, so hidden-state similarity is necessary and sufficient to explain expert-usage similarity — specialization is a property of the representation space, not the routing architecture — and load-balancing loss provably suppresses shared hidden directions, explaining specialization collapse under less diverse data. The warning side: the symmetry group now includes expert permutations, which breaks your dense-model comparability tools — naive interpolation barriers, checkpoint merging (your annealing-proxy trick needs expert matching first or it averages mismatched experts into mush), and stitching all require an expert-alignment step, and re-basin methods for MoE are immature. Budget for that; it’s solvable (routing-log-based expert matching is a natural approach) but it’s real work, and it’s also — characteristically — a contribution in itself, since “how to quotient MoE symmetries for checkpoint comparison” is an open gap.
		Practical cautions before committing: (1) MoE multiplies exactly the kind of from-scratch-tuned knobs your own critique targets — aux-loss coefficients, top-k, expert count, capacity factors are all folklore-tuned at large scale and may be mis-set for 20–50M active, so your regime-mismatch argument applies to your *own* baseline and argues for starting from FLAME-MoE’s validated configs (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81c38d82d8bd8f4ad47f"/>) rather than tuning fresh. (2) Tiny-scale noise: Signal-and-Noise (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>) found noise worsens as scale shrinks, and routing discreteness plausibly adds eval variance — so the noise-floor stage isn’t skippable here, it’s more necessary. (3) Keep a dense control ladder at matched active params, because every MoE-specific finding needs the “is this MoE or is this just small models” comparison, and DataDecide’s small dense models (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) give it to you for free. The attractive endgame this sets up: your matched-loss recipe comparisons run on FLAME-MoE-style models where the *routing fingerprint* is part of the movement profile — and if pretraining recipes at matched loss produce measurably different routing-commitment schedules that predict post-training or elicitation behavior, that’s your thesis phenomenon with its mechanism visible to the naked eye.

	**Same checkpoint, different eval seeds: mostly deterministic — which is both good news and the key subtlety.** Decompose “eval noise” into its actual sources: (a) *Inference nondeterminism* — for the loglikelihood/rank-classification evals DataDecide (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) uses (OLMES-style (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f814690a8f496334f818e"/>)), there’s no sampling; the only randomness is floating-point non-associativity from batching/kernel scheduling, which can flip a rare borderline item but is typically negligible. Re-evaling the same checkpoint with a different “seed” gives you nearly identical numbers. (b) *Generation-based evals* (anything sampled at T\>0) do vary with seed — but that’s not most of the DataDecide (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) suite. (c) *Configuration variance* — few-shot demo choice, demo order, prompt template. This is large, but it’s *systematic*, not random: it shifts all models coherently, so it’s a bias axis to sweep, not noise to average. The upshot: you can’t buy variance reduction on a fixed checkpoint by re-evaling with new seeds, because almost all of the variance you care about isn’t in the eval — it’s in *training* (seed, data order, init). That’s exactly how Signal and Noise (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>) carves it: their noise is the model’s own step-to-step wander, a benchmark’s sensitivity to random variability between training steps, not measurement error at a fixed point.
		Which sharpens your n=3 worry correctly: 3 training seeds is indeed too few to estimate per-recipe seed variance directly. Three mitigations, all cheap. First, **pool**: assume (and then test) that seed-variance is roughly homoscedastic across recipes at fixed scale — 25 recipes × 3 seeds gives you \~50 degrees of freedom for a *shared* variance estimate, plenty, and the test for heteroscedasticity across recipes is itself interesting (a recipe whose seeds diverge more is a finding, not a nuisance). Second, **use the trajectory as replicate** — this is Signal and Noise’s own trick (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>): variability across a window of late checkpoints within one run approximates run-to-run noise at a fraction of the cost, and it’s exactly the quantity your drift/diffusion decomposition would put on principled footing (their windowed noise estimate assumes the drift within the window is negligible; your decomposition checks and corrects that assumption). Third, **bootstrap over items** for the orthogonal axis — benchmark-composition uncertainty from a single run — which costs nothing and is routinely omitted. With those three, n=3 stops being a blocker for anything except recipe-specific variance claims, which you’d flag as the one thing the suite genuinely can’t support.
	**IRT on the DataDecide matrix: yes, and you’ve spotted a real opening.** The setup is ideal: hundreds of model×checkpoint rows, thousands of item columns, binary (and margin) outcomes — precisely the data shape item response theory was built for. Prior art exists but hasn’t been pointed here: IRT for NLP benchmarks (Lalor et al. (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81588b16ffb2c1ea8cb4"/>); Rodriguez et al.’s “Evaluation Examples Are Not Equally Informative” (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f8148b63efdfe60dce3e1"/>)), tinyBenchmarks (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81888d3cf04f3ec4a02b"/>) (Polo et al. — IRT-selected \~100-item subsets that preserve full-benchmark rankings), and metabench (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f811b959bc0ebffa296c6"/>). What they all did is fit IRT to *diverse converged models* to compress benchmarks. What the DataDecide setting (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/f70134021fae4238980fde6583de1bcf"/>) adds is *structure in the model axis*: the rows aren’t arbitrary models, they’re organized trajectories (recipe × scale × seed × step), which converts IRT from a compression tool into a measurement instrument for your program:
	- **Ability θ(t) as the movement metric.** IRT’s latent ability is a continuous scalar extracted from binary outcomes, pooling information across items weighted by their discrimination — it should be substantially lower-noise than accuracy on the same items, i.e., a principled version of the Signal-and-Noise (<mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3c3de135cd1f81118afade26ffa9bc8e"/>) intervention of switching to better-signal metrics and filtering noisy subtasks (their filtering is implicit zero/one weighting; IRT estimates the weights). Testable immediately: is SNR(θ trajectories) \> SNR(accuracy trajectories) on their own released data?
	- **Differential item functioning (DIF) is your matched-loss comparison, formalized.** DIF asks: do items behave differently across model groups *at the same ability level*? Substitute “recipe” for group and you have the psychometric statement of “pretraining data shapes models beyond final performance.” Items exhibiting recipe-DIF are the loci where recipes differ irreducibly — and the natural follow-up is whether DIF items cluster by your token-determinism buckets or by domain, connecting the eval layer back to the data-featurization layer.
	- **Item characteristic curves against compute give per-item emergence points** — the θ (or loss) at which each item becomes solvable — turning “emergence” from a benchmark-level claim into a distribution over items, and giving your proxy-metrics thread a per-item version of the loss-to-accuracy mapping.
	- **Dimensionality is the honest first check, and it’s substantive rather than procedural:** classical IRT assumes one latent dimension; if a single θ fits the DataDecide matrix well, that’s evidence recipes at these scales differ mostly along one axis (and matched-loss ≈ matched-everything, deflating part of your hypothesis at this scale!); if it demands multiple dimensions, the factor structure *is* the answer to “what do recipes change besides final performance.” Either outcome is a result.
	Two cautions: 
	- IRT’s local-independence assumption is violated by shared-passage items and by contamination (fit diagnostics will flag these — also useful); 
	- binary IRT discards the margin information that carries most small-scale signal, so fit both binary and continuous-response variants (on likelihood margins) and compare
	- the comparison itself replicating Signal and Noise’s metric-choice finding inside a single framework.
	- As a project shape, this is another all-inference, public-data, weeks-scale artifact: “a psychometric reanalysis of DataDecide” slots between your Stage-0 movement decomposition and the recipe questions, and produces the calibrated instrument (θ trajectories, item bank with difficulty/discrimination/DIF flags) that every later stage would rather use than raw accuracy.
